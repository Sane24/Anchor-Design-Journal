CS 160 · Summer 2026 · UC Berkeley · Team Hi-Five Senses

When everything feels due at once, nothing gets started.

So we built Anchor , an app that gathers a scattered day from Gmail and Calendar, narrows it to three tasks , and hands you one small first step for each. It is built for the overwhelm and the paralysis that come with a scattered day, not just the trouble of starting. This page walks through how it got made, from seven interviews to a working app in six weeks.

01 · Needfinding · seven sessions, in the wild

Watching people not start

We observed seven students and recent graduates managing ADHD, anxiety, or depression as they began real work in their own spaces, then interviewed them about habits, stress, and workarounds. Every one of them kept plans, lists, and deadlines, and still had trouble beginning. The barrier we kept seeing was initiation and overwhelm . The tools participants already used helped them organize work, but rarely lowered the barrier to beginning it. We designed for people at the sharp end of this problem, with the hypothesis that these needs could also inform tools for anyone facing an overloaded day.

A call put off for a year

One participant had everything ready to call her eye doctor: the number, a written script, her insurance card. The call sat undone for a year. When we shrank the task to “just find the number,” she finished in five minutes, no distractions.

The call itself was never the hard part. It had no scheduled start and no clear end, so it never began. Once the task had edges, it happened.

Tasks without edges don’t begin.

Open-ended tasks sat undone for months, even with every prerequisite ready.

Downscoping works.

Shrinking a scary task to a five-minute version was enough to make it happen.

Self-report feels fake.

One participant abandoned Habitica because checking her own boxes killed motivation instead of building it.

People manufacture urgency.

Fake deadlines (“I’ll call at three”), a brother sitting beside them, rewards gated behind chores.

Ambiguity amplifies stress.

Large, unstructured assignments created more overwhelm than work with a visible starting point.

→ Seven POV statements became 13 how-might-we questions and 14 rough solution ideas.

Pro-con lists funneled the sketchbook down to three candidate concepts, developed in more detail:

CONCEPT A

Grayscale distractions

The longer you stay on a distracting app, the grayer your whole phone becomes. Essential apps keep their color.

Why we passed: one participant put it plainly: “it’s not just distraction, it’s that you don’t want to do it.”

OUR PICK
CONCEPT B

Unified briefings

Pull scattered obligations from Gmail and Calendar into one bounded plan: three tasks a day, each with a tiny first step, closed out by an evening reflection that rolls unfinished work forward.

Why it won: it aims at the barrier our interviews kept surfacing, initiation and overload, and it gives open-ended tasks the edges that make starting possible.

CONCEPT C

Accountability companion

Shared goals and streaks with friends, plus an AR mode for studying side-by-side in a virtual workspace.

Why we passed: social pressure risks the shame and rejection sensitivity that make people abandon these systems in the first place.

03 · Lo-fi prototyping · 2 paradigms × 3 testers each

Paper first

We built two paper prototypes with the same functionality and opposite personalities, then ran think-aloud sessions: plan the day, start the next action, close out and roll over.

WHAT SURVIVED TESTING
KEEP top three · tiny next steps · focus sprint · rollover
CUT / OPTIONAL mood + energy tracking
FIX “where do I start?” · missing due times · false affordances

A won. Consolidation, three tasks, and the tiny first step all landed with testers. But they also flagged an ambiguous starting point, mood tracking nobody asked for, and buttons that looked tappable but weren’t. Into the next round: open on today’s briefing by default, make wellness tracking optional, and attach times to tasks.

One lesson remained unresolved: paper-prototype testers already asked for a guided first run, but the shipped evaluation later surfaced the same onboarding problem again. Identifying a usability issue did not guarantee that it made it into the next build.

04 · Hi-fi prototyping · 3 directions, 6 external critiques

One screen, three ways

Three of us independently designed the same screen in three visual directions, critiqued them as a group, and carried one forward into a full interactive Figma prototype.

The Figma prototype

Design 2 grew into a clickable prototype covering the full daily loop. Six outside participants clicked through it on their own; you can open it in Figma too.

“Green is nice, but…”

5 of 6 testers wanted visual customization; one called color choice “a huge deal maker or breaker.” We planned selectable accent themes.

A stiff timer.

4 of 6 wanted custom durations instead of +5-minute taps, and a wider range of ambient sounds. “Maybe the sounds of oceans?”

Confusing copy.

“Anchor 1 is next” puzzled people, rollover tasks needed explaining, and done tasks didn’t look done.

The identity landed here too: the name Anchor , a mark that is half anchor, half sprout, and the tagline steady the day : a small set of priorities to hold onto, and a garden that grows when you do.

05 · Implementation · React + Supabase + Google APIs

Making it real

Anchor shipped as a React 18 single-page app on GitHub Pages: Supabase for accounts and sync, an offline-first data layer (usable without signing in), and read-only Google Calendar and Gmail access that feeds the morning briefing. Gmail is deliberately filtered down to task-like mail, since surfacing a whole inbox would add to the exact noise the app is meant to cut.

Privacy shaped the implementation as well as the interface. Google access is read-only, Gmail candidates exclude Promotions and Social, and the app remains usable without an account through local storage. Signed-in data syncs through Supabase, where row-level security limits each user to their own records. Because OAuth and token handling happen entirely in the browser, this client-only architecture is practical for a course prototype but would require a dedicated security review before broader deployment.

The accent themes testers asked for

Five of six Figma testers wanted more than green. Sage stayed the default, and Lavender, Ocean, and Terracotta shipped alongside it in Settings, recoloring buttons, highlights, and tabs across the whole app.

Field notes from testing our own build

  • FIXED Postponed tasks vanished: the date changed locally but was never re-saved to the store.
  • FIXED Refreshing mid-sprint reset the focus timer. It now persists its remaining time on every tick.
  • FIXED Pausing the timer didn’t pause the music, so the ambient audio kept playing over a stopped countdown.
  • OPEN Stress testing caught a timezone bug: sample dates generated in UTC land on the wrong day for users east of Greenwich. Fix planned.
A note from testing

The Garden turns your effort into a place.

Testers liked the visual progress and metaphor depicted here, and the only real complaint was that they found it last.

06 · User evaluation · 5 participants, think-aloud

User evaluation

We handed the working app to five participants and read them three scenarios: plan the day, start the next action, and close out the day. We recorded screens, counted taps, and only stepped in after someone had been stuck for 30 seconds. A short survey and a debrief chat followed each session.

P1 recent education sciences grad, manages anxiety P2 Master of Design student, mid thesis crunch P3 recent computer science grad, problems with focus P4 new-grad software engineer, manages anxiety P5 recent economics grad
Our words got in the way.

Labels described the app’s own vocabulary. “Choose an anchor” stalled people, and three of five misread “Start 5 min” as the length of the task.

Silent, one-way actions.

No way out of a finished timer, no undo for “I started,” no signal when a session ended or tasks were cleared.

The best parts hid.

The Garden and Reflection drew the warmest reactions, and they were the last things people found, sometimes only when prompted.

“

I just do what this app does in my head, so this would help reduce mental load.

participant 4 · new-grad engineer managing anxiety

What the next iteration fixes

  1. A guided first run that walks the whole daily loop once with sample content. The app already makes sense on second use, so the goal is to deliver that second-run experience the first time.
  2. Familiar, action-oriented labels such as “Choose 3 tasks” and “Start a 5-minute focus session,” keeping Anchor as the product name rather than requiring users to learn new interface vocabulary.
  3. Reversible, confirmed actions: an early-finish and exit control for the timer, editable task states, visible completion feedback, and undo after clearing tasks.
  4. Optional sound and vibration when a sprint ends, plus morning and evening notifications that open the briefing and reflection directly.
  5. Due dates on task rows, editable in place, with missed deadlines rolled forward as overdue instead of disappearing.

What this evaluation cannot tell us yet

This was a formative usability study with five participants completing guided scenarios in 30–45 minute sessions. It shows where the interface helps or blocks first-time use; it does not yet demonstrate that Anchor reduces procrastination, stress, or task abandonment over time. Our samples across the project were small convenience samples, Google OAuth testing was limited to approved accounts, and a timezone edge case remained open at evaluation. A longitudinal field study with a broader participant group is the next step for testing real-world impact.

07 · Future work

Longer-horizon explorations

After addressing the immediate usability findings above, we would explore ways for Anchor’s briefings and next-step prompts to reach people without requiring another deliberate app check.

Anchor on smart glasses.

Connect with Meta AI glasses so the morning briefing and gentle next-step nudges arrive in your field of view, no phone required.

Spoken briefings.

An audio version of the morning briefing for low-energy mornings, when even opening and reading a screen is friction.

Briefings that come to you.

Morning and evening notifications that open the briefing and reflection directly, on the phone or straight into the glasses.
