Distinct realization 1: Turn distracting apps to gray-scale, and keep the essential apps in color.
Users can select the apps that they think are distracting. The app button and interfaces of the rest
of the apps keep colorful, but the longer the users stay on the distraction apps, the grayer the

1. What makes this solution unique enough to be explored?
Fixes the issue of sense of invasion and boredom of other discipline apps. It serves as a gentle
and intelligent accountability partner 24/7 rather than a cold-hearted officer. This can attract and
maintain more users because it’s not aggressive like other discipline apps to set a time or lock the
app. So users will feel less irritated or intimidated by the function, so users will be less likely to
give up on this anti-addiction journey. The color transitions make users feel more comfortable
and can be a natural reminder of phone use. It can build longer and more stable relationships
with users.
2. What modalities are in-play here?
Mobile App first (larger user base), might extend to Web later
3. What are some pros and some cons for each of these?
Pros are people don’t need to mentally prepare for any sudden app shut down. The grayscale is
much more gradual than cold turkey quitting. People don’t need to learn anything to use this app,
so the method can build relationships with a lot of users. Cons are people might build tolerance
towards the grayscale.
Distinct realization 2: Unified weekly, morning and night briefings that turn scattered

1. What makes this solution unique enough to be explored?
It doesn’t add another to-do list. It pulls obligations already scattered across Gmail, Slack, and
Calendar into one bounded plan and keeps narrowing the day down to a single next action,
closing the loop from a morning briefing to a night reflection. That directly targets the “I feel like
something is due” dread and the initiation gap our interviews surfaced, where the barrier was
never information or planning, it was starting. Instead of requiring users to repeatedly check
several platforms and remember scattered obligations, the system automatically consolidates
them and helps the user turn them into a manageable daily plan.
2. What modalities are in-play here?
Mobile App, Web App, Audio. A mobile or web dashboard, integrations with Gmail, Slack, and
Google Calendar, and optional phone notifications. Audio is included because a spoken morning
briefing removes the “open and read on a screen” step on low-energy mornings where friction
might be the highest.
3. What are some pros and some cons for each of these?
Consolidating Gmail, Slack, and Calendar into one bounded plan for the day, with a single next
action directly targets the “something is due” anxiety dread/overwhelm and the initiation gap
from our interviews, and it meets users in tools they already use daily. The con is that it needs
broad account access and reliable due-date parsing which are both hard and privacy-sensitive.

4. Final Design Selection

-- 17 of 30 --

We chose Unified Briefings (Distinct Realization 2 from Step 3) because the idea targets the
actual barrier our interviews surfaced which is initiation and cognitive overload, not information
or planning. One participant had every prerequisite ready for a year (number found, script
written, insurance card saved) and still couldn’t begin because the task had no bounded start or
end. By pulling scattered obligations from Gmail, Slack, and Calendar into one plan and
narrowing each day to a single tiny next action, this design gives open-ended tasks the edges that
make starting possible, meets people in tools they already use, and closes the loop from morning
to night with rollover so no one opens a blank page. This also prevents the analysis paralysis of
having too many things to do so you don’t know where to start. The other two realizations solve
narrower problems: grayscale apps address distraction, but our participant was clear that “it’s not
just distraction, it’s that you don’t want to do it,” and the buddy system leans on social
accountability, which risks the shame and rejection sensitivity that make ADHD and anxiety
users abandon systems in the first place.
5. User Tasks
1) Plan today from scattered inputs. Start briefing and gather tasks from
Gmail/Slack/Calendar. Goal: go from “something’s due somewhere at some time” to a
locked top 3 or today, each with a first step. Success: 3 missions chosen, each showing
one tiny next step.
2) Start the next action. Goal: find and begin the single smallest next step for the priority
task, without having to decide what to do. Success: user starts the opener.
3) Close out the day and roll over. Goal: log a quick mood, energy, and care check, mark
what got done, carry unfinished work forward. Success: reflection saved, unfinished tasks
appear in tomorrow's plan.
6. Paper Prototypes
Prototype A: Stepped briefing and dashboard with structured screens, one decision per screen.
Interface. A mobile app with one main full-screen view per stage of the day. Each screen is a
dense, scannable surface: the weekly briefing shows source tags (mail, slack, calendar) above a
day-grouped list of obligations, the morning briefing groups today’s items into due, calendar, and
carried-over sections, pick-top-3 is a selectable list, today’s missions shows the three chosen
tasks each with a tiny next step, and night reflection combines mood and energy dot-scales, care
anchors, a done count, and a tomorrow list. Everything on a screen is visible at once, and the user
reads and taps directly.
Navigation flow. Linear and forward-moving, screen to screen: This Week → Morning Briefing
→ Pick top 3 → Today’s 3 Missions → Timer → Night Briefing & Reflection.

How they differ
The main difference between the two prototypes is how users interact with the system and who
drives the experience. Prototype A is a sequence of dedicated screens where information is
grouped by stage (weekly overview, morning briefing, task selection, today’s mission, and night
reflection). Users are given free navigation as they move between pages, selecting items from
lists, and tapping buttons to progress through the workflow. By emphasizing visual organization,
this prototype allows for greater sense of control over the information but can have a higher risk
of an overwhelming interface.
Prototype B replaces these screens with a single conversational interface. Instead of displaying
all information at once, the assistant introduces one decision at a time through a chat thread using
text, quick-reply chips, optional voice input, and audio playback. Rather than navigating between
pages, users respond to prompts as the conversation progresses. This format is intended to

decrease an overwhelming experience and make audio and the reflection feel natural, at the cost
of feeling slower or more hand-holding.

urgency and information about the tasks to be completed.

- Users hope to schedule with notification, location and time.
- He prefers to see today’s tasks page first when opening the app.
- Users emphasized the need for urgency when it comes to the deadline, suggesting adding
alerts before the due time.

8. Analysis & Reflection
Major patterns:
Prototype A (App Briefing dashboard):
● Users praised the surface and its visual design.
● They liked seeing everything laid out, and the top-three framing that narrows the day.
● The wins clustered on helping with initiation and consolidation, with the strongest
response from the tester whose core struggle is starting.
● The problems were mainly on two things: users didn’t know where to begin, and the
day’s tasks lacked specific times.
● Mood and energy tracking was a recurring unwanted feature.
Prototype B (Conversational Co-Pilot).
● Users like the idea of starting from small first.
● Maintenance burden concern
● Information Overload
● Not competitive enough to encourage users to prioritize this method over their current
methods
Common Patterns: Across both, the core idea (consolidate scattered obligations, narrow to a next
action) consistently landed, while the failure modes were about how much the app shows and
asks. Users value simplicity and easy navigation while under the circumstances of dealing with
tasks.
Success:
1. Consolidation. Putting multiple scattered obligations/tasks into one place was appreciated
by nearly everyone (P1, P2, P5, P6), and directly answered the overwhelm with
multi-device and “something is due” scatter anxiety.
2. Only 3 tasks to begin with at one time. Building more confidence and less anxious
overthinking rather than seeing the whole big tasks in front of them. (P1, P2, P3, P4)
3. Focus sprint. Getting in the mood without thinking too much. (P1, P2, P3)
4. Rollover tasks. Carrying unfinished tasks to tomorrow automatically helps memory and
reduces forgotten task anxiety (P1, P5).
5. Tiny next steps. Breaking work into a small next action was said to be helpful in making
daunting tasks manageable (P3, P4).
6. Clarity of the copilot chat flow. The chat sequencing made it clear that outputs follow the
user’s input (P4, P6), and users noted that it would help anyone, not only people with
focus struggles.

Common usability issues:
1. Start confusion/ambiguity. P1 (“what am I supposed to do”), P2 (“not sure what to
press”), P3 (start ambiguity).
2. Timer gaps, minor but a recurring request. P1 and P3 wanted do-not-disturb, notification
control, and a visual cue at five minutes left.
3. Mood and Energy tracking not wanted, moderate and recurring (P2, P3, P5). Focus-only
users did not want to track feelings.
4. No due time within today (P1).
5. Pick 3 and Today’s 3 missions look alike (P3).
6. False affordances with buttons, minor. P3 pressed the Gmail icon in the weekly overview
screen expecting Gmail-specific tasks, because the source icon reads as a button.
7. Text/handwriting was small, so clarifications was asked (P5, P6)
8. Burden of adjusting the task plan (P4, P5)
Planned changes for next iteration:
1. Fix the entry point. Open on today’s morning briefing screen by default (P1).
2. Add a guided first run for better affordance, and make one obvious “start here” action.
3. Make the gmail/calendar/slack connections into buttons that show specific information
from those platforms.
4. Remove mood and energy tracking or make it optional. Add trends related to sleep,
movement and tasks completed, so the health tracking in the reflection screen feels
useful.
5. Differentiate the two selection screens (Pick 3 vs Today’s 3 Missions) visually, or
improve the flow to make it more intuitive.
6. Add time to tasks. Per-task due times, scheduling with time and optional location, and
alerts before the deadline.
7. Add an option to zoom in and out with text in the conversational co-pilot, and add a
pause and rest option.
8. Personalize integrations to what the users actually use. P2 doesn’t use Slack, so showing
a slack logo on the main screen was unnecessary information.
