Project Milestone 5: User Evaluation
1) 3 User Scenarios
Primary User Task 1: Plan today from scattered inputs
● Milestone 2: Start a briefing and gather tasks from Gmail/Calendar. Goal: go from
“something's due somewhere at some time” to a locked top 3 for today, each with a first
step.
● Scenario read to participant: “You’ve just woken up and realize you have a full day of
classes/work, an assignment due, a midterm coming up, or a pile of unread emails. You
want to walk into the day with a clear head instead of scrolling through everything that’s
pending. Show me how you’d use this app to figure out what actually matters today and
lock in your plan.”
● Success criteria: participant starts morning briefing, is able to see upcoming tasks or adds
tasks manually, selects 3 Anchors for the day; process is intuitive and does not take up
too much time.
Primary User Task 2: Start the next action
● Milestone 2: Find and begin the single smallest next step for the priority task, without
having to decide what to do.
● Scenario read to participant: “You’ve made a plan for your day and identified these three
main Anchors. However, you’re having trouble getting started as it feels like there’s so
much to do for these Anchors. Using this app, show me how you would break down the
task and actually sit down and begin working on your first Anchor in a Focus Session.”
● Success criteria: participant starts the opener/first step for their top Anchor (e.g., begins a
focus session) and knows the first step to begin without pausing to decide what to do.
Primary User Task 3: Close out the day and roll over
● Milestone 2: Mark what got done, carry unfinished work forward. Success: reflection
saved, unfinished tasks appear in tomorrow's plan.
● Scenario read to participant: “It’s the end of the day. You got through some of what you
planned, but not all of it. Show me how you’d use this app to wrap up and see what you
got done as well as decide what happens to what’s left.”
● Success criteria: participant saves a reflection, and any unfinished Anchor is confirmed to
roll into tomorrow’s plan.

2) Evaluation Interview Protocol
Total session length: 30–45 minutes. Sessions will be screen-recorded (with audio) with the
participant’s consent, and one team member should take live notes while another facilitates.
Recording: use the phone’s built-in screen recorder with microphone enabled
● iPhone: Settings → Control Center → add Screen Recording if not already added. Swipe
into Control Center, long-press the record icon (not a single tap), enable the microphone
option, then Start.
● Android: swipe down twice for Quick Settings → Screen Recorder. Enable microphone
audio before starting
● Maybe a second phone or laptop webcam angled at the participant’s face captures
non-verbal cues (frowns, hesitation) for the qualitative notes?
Introduction and Consent (3 minutes)
“Hi, thanks for taking the time to test this with us today! We're students building an app called
Anchor, meant to help people cut through decision fatigue by narrowing each day down to just
three focus tasks.”
“We're going to give you a phone with the app open, and walk you through a few everyday
situations.This session should take about 30 to 45 minutes total. As you go, please think aloud as
much as possible. Feel free to say whatever’s going through your head. There’s no need to
explain or narrate perfectly, just talk the way you’d think to yourself.”
“This is a test of the app, not of you. If anything is confusing or doesn’t work the way you
expect, that's exactly what we want to find out, and it’s on us, not you. We’ll be screen recording
with audio so we can review it afterward, and everything stays private within our team. Is that
okay with you?”
[Get verbal and signed consent to record.]

Three Usability Themes
Theme 1: Anchor’s labels describe the app’s own concepts rather than the action they
perform, so users cannot predict what a certain button will do.
Codes: A (invented terms), B (duration as label), C (ambiguous check affordance)
3 participants stalled on at least one label, and in each case the confusion was about what the label meant
and what the outcome would be.
A. P4 stopped at “choose an anchor,” did not recognize the word as meaning “task,” and suggested
renaming it. P5 questioned “What’s today’s anchors section?” and later paused at “Start weekly briefing,”
not reading it as an action they could take. P2 questioned whether a feature was even part of the flow:
“Focus session…? Is that part of this?”
B. “Start 5 min” label was the most consistent misread in the user study, across three participants. P5:
“What does start in five minutes mean? It takes five minutes to do?” P1 said both possible readings
without being able to choose between them: “Is the time like a reminder for you in 25 minutes you should
start this task or is it this task will be 25 minutes long, and you’re setting the timer?” and separately
guessed “Maybe it’s telling you how long the task is?” P4 also paused at the same button, unsure whether
it started the task itself or only a timer.
C. Some button’s outcomes were uncertain. P1 hovered over a task circle and asked “If I click this, would
it be accomplished or?” P4 also hesitated at the same control, unsure what state a tap would commit her
to.

The language/terms used that gives this Anchor app its identity is the same language that slows use for
first-time users, and the problem is mostly at the moment of initiation, which is also the moment our
target users already struggle with.
Insight: To make the app more usable, we should use terms the user is already familiar with, like simply
using “Choose 3 tasks” rather than “Choose 3 Anchors.”
Theme 2: Lack of clear app-status feedback, and ability to undo or back out
Codes: D (trapped in the timer), E (irreversible state), F (silent action, no end signal), I (missing time
structure)
This theme rests most heavily on P4, who is our closest match to the target user.
D. P4 finished a focus session early and found no way to end it, then no way to leave the timer
screen at all. She also noted the timer overlay blocks the bottom of the screen, removing her
navigation while she was already stuck.
E. She tried to reverse the “I started” state and stopped when nothing responded, and later
wanted to edit a task after marking it started, with no path to do so.
F. She cleared her tasks and could not tell whether the action had taken effect. The same absence
of signal appeared at the end of a session as well, where P1 asked “Will it ring when it’s over?”
and said “It might feel like an extra step because I use Google Calendar or personal alarms, so
it’s nice to have the actual physical sound like vibration…for this one, you have to check your
phone to see when time is up.” P5 went looking for a sound or alarm setting that did not exist.
I. Beyond the session itself, time is largely invisible. P1 asked for it twice: “When adding your
task, adding a specific time so they’re like in order would be better” and “Alarm setting or
something when you’re tasked, I think adding a time factor like 10 minutes pretty much to be
your reminder part.” P5 wanted the This Week strip to show both weekday and date: “having
both the date but also the day of the week could be useful, just like referencing.” P4 asked for
due dates on task rows and the ability to extend them.
Insight: For a user group whose central difficulty is starting, a system that makes a premature or
mistaken start irreversible or silent would lower the chance of them trying at all.
Theme 3: The features users valued most were the ones they discovered last

H. P5 on the Garden: “The garden section, I didn;t know what it was until I looked at it more. I

M. The first-step suggestion is the one differentiator 3 users encountered immediately, and it was

5) Next Steps & Future Iterations
1. Add a guided walkthrough that teaches the daily loop on first open
Finding: Every participant needed a second pass before the app made sense (Code H). P4 said
“It’s a lot more intuitive trying it the second time.” P5 said “generally just getting used to how
everything works together, it’s organized, it just takes a bit of getting used to.” P3 took roughly
two minutes to discover he could set a date while adding a task, ending with “It took me around
2 minutes to figure out this, but now I feel good about it.” P4 never opened Reflection on her
own, partly because nothing prompted her to (Code G).
Change: Add a short first-run walkthrough that runs the full loop once with sample content:
morning briefing, pick three, start a session, then reflection. The app already succeeds on second
use, so the goal is to deliver the second-run experience on the first run.
2. Rename product-specific labels to familiar action words
Finding: All five participants stalled on at least one label (Code A). P4 did not know what
“choose an anchor” meant and suggested “choose a task.” P5 asked “What’s the today’s anchors
section?” and did not read “Start weekly briefing” as an action.
Change: Rename “Anchor” to “Task” or “Today’s 3” in the interface, keeping “Anchor” as the
app’s name rather than its vocabulary. Rewrite section headers as actions (“Plan my week”
instead of “Start weekly briefing”). Retest whether new users can state what each button does
before pressing it.
3. Make every action reversible and confirmed
Finding: Codes D, E, F. P4 finished a session early with no way to end it and no way to leave the
timer screen, tried to undo “I started” and got no response, and cleared her tasks without being
able to tell whether it worked. The timer overlay also blocked the bottom of the screen while she
was stuck.
Change: Add a “Done” and an exit control to the timer, make “I started” tappable to undo and
the task editable afterward, and add a confirmation message plus undo option when tasks are
cleared. Keep navigation visible during a session.

not in it

Finding: Three participants went looking for an end of session signal that did not exist (Code F).

Change: This was initially a design choice to not add an alarm so as to not pull a user out of a

5. Show due dates on tasks, and let users edit deadlines and catch missed ones
Finding: Three participants wanted time attached to tasks (Code I). P1 asked twice: “When
adding your task, adding a specific time so they’re in order would be better.” P5 wanted clearer
date referencing: “having both the date but also the day of the week could be useful, just like
referencing.” P3 wanted calendar context while entering tasks: “When I’m editing this (tasks),
part of me wants to see the calendar…cuz it’s easier to add.”
P4 wanted due dates shown on task rows and the ability to extend them if they received an
extension on an assignment. P4 also noticed that missed deadlines that you never added did not
rollover.
Change: Show the due date/time on each task row, and let users edit or extend it in place, and
also give them the ability to reorder tasks. Roll missed deadlines forward into the next day’s
briefing rather than letting them disappear silently, flagged as overdue so the user decides
whether to reschedule or drop them. Show weekdays and date together in “This Week” screen.
